# Diagnostic Framework: Conflict Mediation in Organizational Intelligence

Status of this section: this is a proposed diagnostic heuristic and prototype instrument for theory-building. It has not been empirically validated. The constructs below are model definitions unless otherwise marked. They should be used to generate case-study codes, interview prompts, survey items, and future empirical propositions, not as a finished psychometric scale.

## 1. Purpose and Scope

We propose a diagnostic framework for examining whether a high-stakes decision episode is intelligently mediating conflict among appetite, feasibility, and constraint. A decision episode is a bounded sequence of communications, commitments, reviews, escalations, and actions through which an organization converts conflicting pressures into an ordered course of action. Appetite refers to the pressure family oriented toward growth, revenue, opportunity capture, resource acquisition, speed, expansion, and competitive advantage. Feasibility refers to the pressure family oriented toward implementational reality: technical, operational, socio-technical, infrastructural, temporal, and coordination constraints. Constraint refers to the plural pressure family oriented toward legitimacy, law, compliance, ethics, reputation, stakeholder claims, customer trust, employee legitimacy, and social permission.

The framework is designed for product and strategy decisions, especially decisions in which action order materially changes the resulting episode state. Examples include $\texttt{ship} ; \texttt{review}$ versus $\texttt{review} ; \texttt{ship}$, $\texttt{sell\_enterprise} ; \texttt{build\_compliance}$ versus $\texttt{build\_compliance} ; \texttt{sell\_enterprise}$, and $\texttt{announce} ; \texttt{verify}$ versus $\texttt{verify} ; \texttt{announce}$. In the light formal model, these are sequence-sensitive action pairs, analogous to non-commutativity but stated in the weaker language of material non-equivalence for a decision episode. The diagnostic question is not simply which pressure family is "right." The question is whether the organization has a mediation protocol for surfacing, communicating, weighting, sequencing, translating, and resolving partially conflicting pressure claims before practically irreversible commitments are made.

This framework should be read alongside systems psychodynamics, behavioral theory of the firm, paradox theory, organizational learning, sensemaking, strategic communication, legitimacy and institutional-complexity theory, and organizational cybernetics. Those literatures already support the general importance of group dynamics, bounded rationality, contradictory demands, information distribution, interpretation, partial goal conflict, plural legitimacy pressure, and diagnostic regulation. The contribution proposed here is narrower: a vocabulary for diagnosing how appetite, feasibility, and constraint are sequenced into organizational commitments under communication limits, unequal power distribution, and practical irreversibility.

## 2. Construct Definitions

The following constructs are proposed analytic categories. They are not claims that organizations literally possess id, ego, or superego structures. The construct set deliberately separates diagnostic dimensions from syndromes. Pressure dominance, mediation capacity, conflict mode, and integrated outcome are dimensions: they describe how an episode is organized. Veto theater, roadmap fantasy, compliance paralysis, and reckless launch behavior are syndromes: recurring patterns that may arise from combinations of dominance, weak mediation capacity, and poor conflict mode. This prevents the framework from becoming only a pathology catalogue. The central thesis is that structured conflict can become organizational intelligence when mediation capacities are strong.

| Level | Constructs | Diagnostic role |
|---|---|---|
| Pressure dominance | Appetite dominance, feasibility dominance, constraint dominance | Identifies whether one pressure family prevents mediated sequencing. Dominance does not mean the pressure is strong; it means the pressure becomes sequence-closing. |
| Mediation capacity | Sequencing competence, feasibility translation, constraint decomposition, protocol authority, truthful escalation, protocol learning | Identifies capacities that allow conflict to become executable, legitimate, order-aware commitments. |
| Conflict mode | Productive conflict, defensive conflict | Distinguishes conflict that improves action from conflict that protects actors, status, or prior decisions. |
| Sequencing pathologies | Veto theater, roadmap fantasy, compliance paralysis, reckless launch behavior | Names recurring syndromes that are useful for case coding but should not be treated as independent dimensions. |
| Integrated outcome | Integrated mediation; trust-aware execution in product/customer-trust contexts | Indicates whether the episode converts appetite, feasibility, and constraint into a viable action sequence. |

| Construct | Definition | Formal-model link | Primary diagnostic contrast |
|---|---|---|---|
| Appetite dominance | A decision-episode pattern in which growth, revenue, speed, opportunity capture, investor pressure, or sales urgency becomes undifferentiated, non-translational, and sequence-closing, preventing feasibility and constraint claims from altering action sequence. Appetite dominance is not strong appetite; it is appetite that prevents mediation. | High relative weight of $U_I$ or appetite claims; high $p_I$; delayed or suppressed $E$ and $S$ messages. | Mediated growth versus growth as default sequence. |
| Feasibility dominance | A decision-episode pattern in which implementational concerns become undifferentiated, non-translational, and sequence-closing, defining the action set so narrowly that appetite and legitimacy claims cannot reshape direction. Feasibility dominance is not strong engineering discipline; it is feasibility that prevents mediation. | High relative weight of $U_E$ or feasibility claims; high $p_E$; feasibility becomes agenda-setting rather than translational. | Reality translation versus implementational closure. |
| Constraint dominance | A decision-episode pattern in which legitimacy-preserving pressure becomes undifferentiated, non-translational, and sequence-closing, rather than decomposing risk into actionable conditions. Constraint dominance is not strong legal, ethical, security, or reputational caution; strong constraint may be appropriate when action should stop. | High relative weight of $U_S$ or constraint claims; high $p_S$; plural constraint collapses into broad inhibition. | Trust governance versus blanket inhibition. |
| Sequencing competence | The capacity to notice when action order matters and to compare candidate sequences before commitment. | $\phi^{\mathrm{seq}}$ identifies high-$\kappa$ action pairs and candidate orderings. | Order-aware mediation versus accidental sequencing. |
| Feasibility translation | The capacity to convert ambition, demand, or opportunity into executable commitments with explicit assumptions about capacity, dependencies, architecture, staffing, and timing. | $E$ claims are surfaced and translated before high-$\rho$ commitments. | Executable ambition versus unsupported promise. |
| Constraint decomposition | The capacity to distinguish legal, compliance, ethical, reputational, stakeholder, customer-trust, and employee-legitimacy concerns and translate them into actionable conditions. | $S$ is decomposed rather than treated as one undifferentiated veto. | Risk-sensitive design versus blanket prohibition. |
| Protocol authority | The degree to which review, escalation, or governance processes can actually alter sequence, scope, timing, or commitment terms. | $\phi^{\mathrm{seq}}$ and $\phi^{\mathrm{resolve}}$ have real decision rights under $p_j$. | Real mediation authority versus ceremonial review. |
| Truthful escalation | The capacity for inconvenient information to travel across hierarchy and function before commitment. | Low $c_j^{\mathrm{truth}}$, adequate $B_j$, tolerable $\ell_j$, and sufficient credibility/verifiability $v_j$. | Timely bad news versus suppressed signals. |
| Protocol learning | The capacity for postmortems and outcome reviews to update future surfacing, communication, weighting, sequencing, or resolution rules. | Organizational memory $H$ changes $\Phi$ after high-$\rho$ or high-$\kappa$ episodes. | Learning protocol versus local repair/blame. |
| Weak feasibility mediation | A pattern in which growth, revenue, or opportunity claims are not translated into realistic implementational plans before commitment. | Low $B_E$, high $\ell_E$, high $c_E^{\mathrm{truth}}$, weak feasibility role in $\Phi$. | Productive translation versus fantasy commitment. |
| Weak constraint internalization | A pattern in which legitimacy-preserving considerations enter late, externally, or ceremonially rather than shaping design, sequence, and commitment terms early. | Low $B_S$, high $\ell_S$, weak $S$ integration in $\Phi$. | Internalized trust work versus late-stage veto/rework. |
| Productive conflict | Conflict that surfaces incompatible pressure claims early enough to improve action sequence, scope, timing, evidence standards, or risk allocation. | $\Phi$ uses disagreement to surface claims, manage high $\rho$ and $\kappa$, and update organizational memory $H$. | Conflict as computation versus conflict as status contest. |
| Defensive conflict | Conflict organized around blame avoidance, face-saving, turf protection, denial, or symbolic compliance rather than action improvement. | High truthful disclosure cost; distorted messages; protocol fails to update after failure. | Learning from conflict versus defending against it. |
| Communication collapse | A condition in which actors carrying relevant pressure claims cannot transmit decision-critical information with sufficient bandwidth, timing, credibility, verifiability, or psychological/political safety. | Low $B_j$, high $\ell_j$, high $c_j^{\mathrm{truth}}$, low $v_j$, and power asymmetry $p_j$. | Limited but useful communication versus non-transmission. |
| Veto theater | A process in which review or approval rituals appear to manage constraint or feasibility, but commitment order has already been fixed by prior action or power distribution. | $S$ or $E$ messages arrive after high-$\rho$ commitment; $\phi^{\mathrm{seq}}$ or $\phi^{\mathrm{resolve}}$ is ceremonial. | Real protocol authority versus staged approval. |
| Roadmap fantasy | A pattern in which plans, customer promises, or board narratives assume capacity, architecture, compliance, or delivery conditions that have not been verified. | Appetite sequence precedes feasibility validation; high unmanaged $\rho$ and $\kappa$. | Ambitious planning versus ungrounded commitment. |
| Compliance paralysis | A pattern in which constraint is treated as an undifferentiated prohibition, causing delay, ambiguity, or avoidance even where decomposed risks could be managed. | Constraint family $S$ is not decomposed; $U_S$ dominates without translation into action options. | Risk-sensitive sequencing versus generalized stoppage. |
| Reckless launch behavior | Repeated shipping, announcement, or sales commitment before feasibility and constraint checks occur for high-consequence decisions. | $I ; E$ or $I ; S$ sequences where $E ; I$ or $S ; I$ would reduce risk; high $\rho$. | Calculated risk-taking versus unmanaged exposure. |
| Integrated mediation | The positive outcome pattern in which appetite, feasibility, and constraint claims are surfaced, weighted, sequenced, translated, and resolved without single-force dominance. | High-quality $\Phi$ across surfacing, communication, weighting, sequencing, and resolution. | Organizational intelligence versus fragmented pressure response. |
| Trust-aware execution | A product-context application of integrated mediation in which the decision episode preserves customer trust, legal safety, technical viability, and strategic momentum. | Integrated mediation before high-$\rho$ product, customer, security, or compliance commitments. | Trust-preserving execution versus brittle delivery. |

## 3. Observable Indicators

The indicators below are intended for qualitative coding of documents, meetings, interviews, and decision records. They are not validated measurement items. In early studies, analysts should treat them as sensitizing indicators and triangulate across evidence types.

| Construct | Observable signs across organizational artifacts and settings |
|---|---|
| Appetite dominance | **Strategy documents:** growth targets without explicit feasibility or trust assumptions; repeated language of speed, capture, or dominance without risk sequencing. **Roadmap meetings:** scope increases despite unresolved engineering or compliance issues. **Sales calls:** commitments precede product, security, or legal confirmation. **Architecture reviews:** technical objections reframed as lack of urgency. **Security reviews:** review begins after launch or enterprise promise. **Board updates:** revenue narrative foregrounded while operational debt is backgrounded. **Postmortems:** failures attributed to execution speed rather than commitment order. **Customer communications:** confident promises outpace readiness. **Hiring/performance systems:** rewards favor bookings, launches, or expansion even when downstream repair costs rise. |
| Feasibility dominance | **Strategy documents:** market options narrowed to current stack, team, or architecture without strategic comparison. **Roadmap meetings:** engineering constraints end discussion rather than generating alternative sequences. **Sales calls:** customer demand is dismissed as "not how the system works" without translation. **Architecture reviews:** elegance, maintainability, or platform purity outweigh validated urgency. **Security reviews:** implementability becomes the main legitimacy test. **Board updates:** risk is described mainly as technical complexity. **Postmortems:** missed opportunities are rationalized as discipline. **Customer communications:** explanations emphasize internal limitations more than customer outcomes. **Hiring/performance systems:** promotion criteria reward technical control while underweighting market responsiveness and trust-building. |
| Constraint dominance | **Strategy documents:** legal, reputational, or compliance exposure described only as reasons not to act. **Roadmap meetings:** ambiguous risks stop decision-making without specifying mitigations. **Sales calls:** customer-facing teams cannot explain what would make a deal permissible. **Architecture reviews:** technical alternatives are blocked by broad policy language. **Security reviews:** review produces open-ended prohibitions rather than conditions for safe progress. **Board updates:** risk posture is reported without option design. **Postmortems:** avoidance is treated as success even when strategic cost is unknown. **Customer communications:** communications become noncommittal or evasive. **Hiring/performance systems:** employees learn that avoiding visible risk is safer than resolving it. |
| Sequencing competence | **Strategy documents:** high-consequence commitments identify order dependencies. **Roadmap meetings:** teams compare review-before-ship, ship-before-review, sell-before-build, and build-before-sell variants. **Sales calls:** commitments are staged by verified readiness. **Architecture/risk reviews:** reviewers flag high-$\kappa$ action pairs. **Board updates:** leaders explain why sequencing choices matter. **Postmortems:** recurrence of order-sensitive failures is tracked. **Customer communications:** timing claims reflect sequencing constraints. **Hiring/performance systems:** judgment about ordering and dependency management is rewarded. |
| Feasibility translation | **Strategy documents:** ambitions include capacity, dependency, staffing, and timing assumptions. **Roadmap meetings:** growth asks are converted into executable slices. **Sales calls:** commercial commitments use implementation conditions. **Architecture reviews:** design alternatives connect technical constraints to strategic options. **Security/risk reviews:** implementation requirements are made concrete. **Board updates:** feasibility is reported as translation work, not obstruction. **Postmortems:** unrealistic commitments change planning rules. **Customer communications:** delivery claims are bounded by verified capability. **Hiring/performance systems:** preventive realism and translation work are rewarded. |
| Constraint decomposition | **Strategy documents:** constraint is separated into legal, compliance, ethical, reputational, stakeholder, customer-trust, and employee-legitimacy concerns. **Roadmap meetings:** risks are translated into conditions for action. **Sales calls:** teams distinguish prohibited, reviewable, allowed, and staged commitments. **Architecture/risk reviews:** trust, auditability, privacy, and safety implications become design requirements. **Board updates:** leaders report risk composition rather than a single red/yellow/green status. **Postmortems:** constraint misses are classified by type. **Customer communications:** trust claims are precise. **Hiring/performance systems:** risk translation is valued. |
| Protocol authority | **Strategy documents:** review gates specify decision rights. **Roadmap meetings:** review findings can alter scope, timing, or sequence. **Sales calls:** approvals are not promised before authorized review. **Architecture/security/risk reviews:** sign-off processes can stop, stage, or reshape commitments. **Board updates:** governance authority is described in practice, not only on slides. **Postmortems:** reviewers are evaluated against authority they actually had. **Customer communications:** approval claims match real review outcomes. **Hiring/performance systems:** accountability is paired with decision rights. |
| Truthful escalation | **Strategy documents:** assumptions and unresolved risks are visible. **Roadmap meetings:** inconvenient information can be raised before dates are fixed. **Sales calls:** customer-facing teams can escalate unsupported commitments. **Architecture/security/risk reviews:** dissent travels without career penalty. **Board updates:** uncertainty and dissent are not laundered out. **Postmortems:** early warnings are examined without punishing the messenger. **Customer communications:** known limitations are disclosed accurately. **Hiring/performance systems:** accurate escalation is rewarded. |
| Protocol learning | **Strategy documents:** prior episode lessons change future decision rules. **Roadmap meetings:** repeated failure modes alter planning criteria. **Sales calls:** deal terms change after promise-risk episodes. **Architecture/security/risk reviews:** checklists and escalation paths are updated after incidents. **Board updates:** leaders report protocol changes, not only local fixes. **Postmortems:** action items modify $\Phi$. **Customer communications:** repair commitments affect future disclosure and delivery practice. **Hiring/performance systems:** learning that prevents recurrence is rewarded. |
| Weak feasibility mediation | **Strategy documents:** targets lack capacity, dependency, reliability, staffing, or architecture assumptions. **Roadmap meetings:** dates are set before work decomposition. **Sales calls:** bespoke promises are made without implementation review. **Architecture reviews:** reviews occur after commitments and become remediation sessions. **Security reviews:** technical control requirements are discovered late. **Board updates:** delivery confidence lacks operational evidence. **Postmortems:** recurring "underestimated complexity" explanations. **Customer communications:** revised dates or narrowed scope after initial certainty. **Hiring/performance systems:** engineers are evaluated on rescuing unrealistic commitments rather than preventing them. |
| Weak constraint internalization | **Strategy documents:** law, ethics, privacy, security, trust, and reputation appear in separate risk sections rather than product logic. **Roadmap meetings:** constraint teams are consulted late or only for approval. **Sales calls:** customer trust requirements are treated as paperwork after commercial agreement. **Architecture reviews:** privacy, auditability, compliance, or abuse-risk requirements are bolted on. **Security reviews:** review is externalized to customer questionnaires or procurement deadlines. **Board updates:** trust and compliance appear after incidents or escalations. **Postmortems:** legal/security/customer-trust issues are classified as surprises. **Customer communications:** disclosures are reactive rather than anticipated. **Hiring/performance systems:** constraint expertise has low status unless a crisis occurs. |
| Productive conflict | **Strategy documents:** competing goals and assumptions are explicit. **Roadmap meetings:** disagreement changes scope, sequencing, or decision criteria. **Sales calls:** customer demand is translated into bounded commitments and escalation paths. **Architecture reviews:** technical objections produce alternative designs or staged commitments. **Security reviews:** findings change design before high-consequence release. **Board updates:** leaders report tradeoffs and protocol changes, not only outcomes. **Postmortems:** lessons alter launch, review, staffing, or deal-desk rules. **Customer communications:** trust is preserved through accurate commitments and timely updates. **Hiring/performance systems:** employees are rewarded for surfacing material risk early. |
| Defensive conflict | **Strategy documents:** tensions are hidden behind consensus language. **Roadmap meetings:** teams argue over ownership or blame rather than action order. **Sales calls:** objections are attributed to other departments. **Architecture reviews:** critique becomes status defense. **Security reviews:** evidence is selected to justify a pre-existing position. **Board updates:** narrative protects leadership credibility more than decision quality. **Postmortems:** root causes remain local and personal; protocols do not change. **Customer communications:** messages minimize responsibility or overpromise repair. **Hiring/performance systems:** employees learn that bad news is punished. |
| Communication collapse | **Strategy documents:** assumptions from one function are invisible to others. **Roadmap meetings:** decision-critical information appears after commitment or not at all. **Sales calls:** customer-facing teams lack credible answers from engineering, legal, or security. **Architecture reviews:** technical implications are too compressed for nontechnical decision-makers. **Security reviews:** reviewers cannot access product context or threat models. **Board updates:** risk summaries omit dissent, uncertainty, or timing constraints. **Postmortems:** teams report that "everyone knew" different facts. **Customer communications:** inconsistent claims across functions. **Hiring/performance systems:** cross-functional translation is not rewarded or staffed. |
| Veto theater | **Strategy documents:** review gates are listed, but escalation authority is unclear. **Roadmap meetings:** approval is sought after date, scope, or launch narrative is fixed. **Sales calls:** customers are promised approval before review has occurred. **Architecture reviews:** review comments cannot change schedule or scope. **Security reviews:** sign-off is pressured as a formality. **Board updates:** governance exists on slides but not in decision rights. **Postmortems:** reviewers are blamed despite lacking early authority. **Customer communications:** "approved" claims mask unresolved concerns. **Hiring/performance systems:** review roles are accountable for outcomes without power to change sequence. |
| Roadmap fantasy | **Strategy documents:** plans depend on unstated capacity, nonexistent controls, or speculative architecture. **Roadmap meetings:** uncertainty is converted into dates without evidence. **Sales calls:** future features are presented as near-certainties. **Architecture reviews:** technical prerequisites are discovered after roadmap publication. **Security reviews:** enterprise controls are promised before implementation. **Board updates:** roadmap confidence exceeds execution evidence. **Postmortems:** repeated slippage is treated as estimation failure rather than promise failure. **Customer communications:** commitments are revised, delayed, or reinterpreted. **Hiring/performance systems:** optimistic planning is rewarded; realism is treated as negativity. |
| Compliance paralysis | **Strategy documents:** constraint appears as a list of blockers rather than design requirements. **Roadmap meetings:** teams avoid decisions because no one can specify acceptable risk. **Sales calls:** customer-facing teams cannot distinguish prohibited, reviewable, and allowed commitments. **Architecture reviews:** compliance concerns prevent alternatives from being explored. **Security reviews:** reviewers demand total certainty before any step. **Board updates:** risk appetite is unspecified. **Postmortems:** missed opportunities are attributed to "compliance" without decomposition. **Customer communications:** vague caution replaces clear trust posture. **Hiring/performance systems:** employees are rewarded for not being associated with risk. |
| Reckless launch behavior | **Strategy documents:** launch timing is justified primarily by market window. **Roadmap meetings:** known defects, security gaps, or trust issues are deferred past release. **Sales calls:** launch claims are used to close deals despite readiness gaps. **Architecture reviews:** concerns are deferred to "post-launch hardening." **Security reviews:** critical findings become launch exceptions. **Board updates:** launch success is measured before downstream repair cost is visible. **Postmortems:** incidents recur after similar sequence choices. **Customer communications:** customers learn about limitations through failures rather than informed disclosure. **Hiring/performance systems:** launch heroics are rewarded more than reliable release practice. |
| Integrated mediation | **Strategy documents:** growth, implementability, and legitimacy assumptions are jointly stated. **Roadmap meetings:** conflict changes action order rather than suppressing one pressure family. **Sales calls:** promises are bounded, sequenced, and reviewable. **Architecture/security/risk reviews:** technical and legitimacy constraints become design inputs. **Board updates:** leaders report tradeoffs and mediation quality. **Postmortems:** protocol updates follow materially from outcomes. **Customer communications:** commitments remain accurate and corrigible. **Hiring/performance systems:** cross-functional mediation is rewarded. |
| Trust-aware execution | **Strategy documents:** growth goals include feasibility, security, compliance, and trust assumptions. **Roadmap meetings:** high-consequence decisions require early cross-functional sequencing. **Sales calls:** promises are bounded by verified capabilities and explicit review paths. **Architecture reviews:** designs include auditability, reliability, and customer-trust implications. **Security reviews:** review informs design before commitments. **Board updates:** leaders report both opportunity and mediation quality. **Postmortems:** protocol changes are tracked and retested. **Customer communications:** commitments are precise, timely, and corrigible. **Hiring/performance systems:** advancement rewards truthful escalation, cross-functional translation, and durable execution. |

Respondents may include executives, product leaders, engineers, legal/compliance/security teams, and customer-facing teams. Items should be answered for a focal decision episode or recurrent episode class, not as a free-floating judgment about the whole organization.

Suggested response scale:

1. Strongly disagree / rarely true
2. Disagree / sometimes true
3. Mixed or uncertain
4. Agree / often true
5. Strongly agree / consistently true

For negatively valenced constructs, high scores indicate higher risk. For positively valenced constructs, high scores indicate healthier mediation. Analysts should not aggregate scores mechanically until future work tests reliability and validity.

## 5. Proposed Qualitative Scoring Model

This scoring model is proposed only as a diagnostic heuristic. It is not validated and should not be used as a standalone performance measure, employee evaluation tool, investment rating, or compliance certification.

### 5.1 Construct-Level Scoring

For each construct, assign a qualitative score using interview evidence, document review, and meeting/process observation for the focal decision episode or episode class.

| Score | Label | Meaning for pathology constructs | Meaning for healthy constructs |
|---:|---|---|---|
| 1 | Low | Rare, weak, or not observed. | Absent or weakly observed. |
| 2 | Low-medium | Occasional and localized. | Present but inconsistent or localized. |
| 3 | Medium | Recurring but not dominant. | Recurring but fragile or uneven. |
| 4 | Medium-high | Frequent and materially shapes decisions. | Frequent and materially improves decisions. |
| 5 | High | Pervasive and protocol-shaping. | Pervasive and protocol-shaping. |

Pathology constructs include appetite dominance, feasibility dominance, constraint dominance, weak feasibility mediation, weak constraint internalization, defensive conflict, communication collapse, veto theater, roadmap fantasy, compliance paralysis, and reckless launch behavior. Positive or neutral mediation-capacity constructs include sequencing competence, feasibility translation, constraint decomposition, protocol authority, truthful escalation, protocol learning, productive conflict, integrated mediation, and trust-aware execution. The table maps positively worded items to positive constructs where possible. Later versions should either avoid reverse-coded items or mark them explicitly with "R" in the item table.

### 5.2 Episode Context Flags

Construct scores should be interpreted in relation to episode severity. A high pathology score matters most when the episode is high-$\rho$, high-$\kappa$, externally exposed, technically coupled, and low-evidence. Conversely, a moderate pathology score in a low-stakes, reversible, internally contained episode may be less important. These flags are proposed context variables, not validated weights.

| Context flag | Diagnostic question | Formal-model link |
|---|---|---|
| Practical irreversibility | How hard is this commitment to undo or repair? | $\rho(s)$ |
| Sequence sensitivity | How much does action order matter? | $\kappa(s,t)$ |
| External exposure | Are customers, regulators, investors, media, partners, or employees exposed? | Episode state $x_t$ and external response $\epsilon_t$ |
| Trust consequence | Could the decision materially affect customer, employee, public, or institutional trust? | Constraint and legitimacy components of $S$ |
| Technical coupling | Could failure propagate through technical, operational, or organizational systems? | Feasibility state and dependency structure |
| Evidence quality | Are key assumptions verified, contested, or speculative? | Verifiability $v_j$ and episode records |
| Time pressure | Is urgency external, constructed, incentive-driven, or strategically chosen? | Appetite pressure, power $p_j$, and communication latency $\ell_j$ |

### 5.3 Suggested Profile Interpretation

The instrument is most useful as a profile, not as a total score. A plausible first-pass profile could report:

| Dimension | Constructs included | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Dominance pattern | Appetite dominance, feasibility dominance, constraint dominance | Which pressure family tends to overdetermine action sequence. |
| Mediation capacity | Sequencing competence, feasibility translation, constraint decomposition, protocol authority, truthful escalation, protocol learning | Whether the episode can turn conflict into executable, legitimate, order-aware commitments. |
| Mediation weakness | Weak feasibility mediation, weak constraint internalization, communication collapse | Whether relevant information fails to alter action before commitment. |
| Conflict quality | Productive conflict, defensive conflict | Whether disagreement improves action or protects actors from accountability. |
| Sequencing pathology | Veto theater, roadmap fantasy, compliance paralysis, reckless launch behavior | Which recurring action-order failures appear. |
| Integrated outcome | Integrated mediation; trust-aware execution in product/customer-trust contexts | Whether growth, implementability, and legitimacy claims are integrated before high-consequence commitments. |
| Episode severity | Practical irreversibility, sequence sensitivity, external exposure, trust consequence, technical coupling, evidence quality, time pressure | How consequential the profile is for the focal episode. |

### 5.4 Prototype Risk Bands

Risk bands should be treated as interpretive aids only.

| Band | Possible pattern | Cautious interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Low pathology / high mediation capacity | Pathology constructs mostly 1-2; mediation-capacity constructs and integrated mediation mostly 4-5. | The episode class may have strong mediation protocols, but external validation would still be needed. |
| Mixed mediation | Several pathology constructs at 3; mediation-capacity constructs at 3-4. | Some episode classes may be mediated well while others fail. Case-level coding is needed. |
| Dominance risk | One dominance construct at 4-5 with mediation weakness at 3-5. | One pressure family may be shaping action sequence disproportionately. |
| Sequence-failure risk | Roadmap fantasy, veto theater, reckless launch behavior, or compliance paralysis at 4-5, especially with high-$\rho$ or high-$\kappa$ flags. | Action order may be a major source of avoidable repair cost. |
| Defensive-system risk | Defensive conflict and communication collapse at 4-5; productive conflict at 1-2. | Information needed for intelligent mediation may be politically or structurally unavailable. |

## 6. Pattern Matrix

The matrix below translates construct profiles into ideal types. These types are heuristic, not exhaustive. They should be read as recurring episode patterns rather than fixed organizational essences. Real organizations may combine several patterns or vary by product line, geography, leadership team, or decision class. The first three rows correspond to the id-like, ego-like, and superego-like metaphor introduced earlier, but the diagnostic framework uses appetite, feasibility, constraint, and mediation protocol as its primary vocabulary.

The protocol adjustments below are not validated interventions. They are theory-derived candidate adjustments for future empirical testing.

| Type | Description | Typical symptoms | Likely failure mode | Candidate protocol adjustment | Related theoretical construct |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Appetite-dominant pattern | Growth, speed, revenue, expansion, or market urgency repeatedly closes the action sequence before feasibility or constraint can alter it. | Sell-before-build commitments; launch-first review; optimistic board narratives; post-launch hardening; rising technical, legal, or trust debt. | Accumulated recovery burden after high-$\rho$ commitments; reputational or operational crisis; customer trust loss. | Require early feasibility and constraint representation for high-consequence actions; create deal-desk and launch protocols with authority to alter sequence; track downstream repair cost. | Exploration/exploitation tension; behavioral search and aspiration pressure; strategic information transmission; practical irreversibility. |
| Feasibility-dominant pattern | Implementational reality becomes sequence-closing rather than translational, narrowing strategic or legitimacy alternatives too early. | Roadmaps shaped by current architecture; customer demand dismissed as technically inconvenient; strategic opportunities delayed until internal systems feel complete. | Missed market windows, low strategic responsiveness, or customer alienation despite operational discipline. | Translate technical constraints into strategic options; create bounded experiments; include customer and trust criteria in architecture decisions. | Socio-technical systems; organizational learning; bounded rationality; exploitation bias. |
| Constraint-dominant pattern | Legitimacy-preserving pressure becomes undifferentiated and sequence-closing rather than decomposed into actionable conditions. | Ambiguous approval processes; excessive caution; inability to define acceptable evidence; customer-facing uncertainty. | Compliance paralysis, strategic stagnation, or informal workarounds that bypass governance. | Decompose constraint into legal, compliance, security, ethical, reputational, stakeholder, customer-trust, and employee-legitimacy components; define risk appetite and staged mitigation paths. | Institutional complexity; legitimacy theory; cybernetic regulation. |
| Weak feasibility translation pattern | Appetite makes commitments that feasibility cannot translate into executable plans. | Chronic underestimation; repeated launch delays; heroic engineering recoveries; technical debt hidden until crisis. | Roadmap fantasy becomes normal; engineering credibility erodes; repair work crowds out strategy. | Add feasibility review before external commitments; require capacity and dependency assumptions in roadmaps; reward preventive realism. | Organizational learning; communication bandwidth; mediation protocol weakness. |
| Weak constraint internalization pattern | Constraint considerations are late, externalized, ceremonial, or politically weak. | Legal/security/privacy/trust issues discovered after sale or launch; customer review drives internal control work; review functions lack authority. | Regulatory, contractual, reputational, or customer-trust exposure; veto theater after commitment. | Internalize trust requirements into product and sales processes; move security/legal/privacy earlier; give review teams authority over high-$\rho$ sequencing. | Institutional logics; legitimacy theory; strategic communication; practical irreversibility. |
| High productive conflict pattern | Disagreement is structured so that it improves scope, order, timing, interpretation, and learning. | Dissent changes decisions; postmortems update protocols; leaders report tradeoffs; cross-functional translation is rewarded. | Possible local delay or decision cost, but lower avoidable repair burden if protocols remain proportionate. | Preserve conflict channels; tune review intensity to decision irreversibility and sequence sensitivity; maintain shared decision records. | Paradox theory; sensemaking; organizational learning; collective intelligence; cybernetic feedback. |
| High integrated mediation pattern | Appetite, feasibility, and constraint claims are surfaced early, translated into comparable commitments, sequenced deliberately, and resolved without single-force dominance. | Explicit assumptions; real review authority; inconvenient information travels; risk is decomposed; postmortems update protocol; commitments remain corrigible. | Main risk is not absence of conflict but over-formalization, delay, or false confidence if the protocol becomes ritualized. | Preserve proportionality: apply heavier mediation to high-$\rho$, high-$\kappa$, externally exposed, low-evidence episodes while keeping low-stakes decisions lightweight. | Mediation protocol quality; organizational learning; cybernetic feedback; practical irreversibility. |
| High defensive conflict pattern | Conflict protects status, identity, or blame positions rather than improving action. | Bad news delayed; meetings become turf contests; postmortems personalize failure; customer messages minimize responsibility. | Communication collapse; repeated failures because protocol does not learn; hidden risk accumulates. | Reduce truthful disclosure costs; separate learning reviews from blame assignment where possible; make protocol changes the expected output of postmortems. | Systems psychodynamics; cheap talk under partial conflict; organizational memory failure; power asymmetry. |
